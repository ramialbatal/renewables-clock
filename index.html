How this is calculated

Official statistics report electricity generation per year, not per second. When this page opens it downloads the latest annual figures for (currently up to ), projects them to using each country's latest year-on-year change ( to ), and spreads the result evenly over the year:

growth   = G[latest] / G[latest - 1] - 1
annual   = G[latest] × (1 + min(growth, 50%))^(this year - latest)
per sec  = annual ÷ seconds in this year
so far   = annual × fraction of the year elapsed (UTC)

Real generation swings with the sun, the wind, the seasons and rainfall, so the live counters are a model, not a measurement. Growth used for the projection is capped at 50% a year, because solar in some countries is growing several-fold from a tiny base and would otherwise run away; the Trend / yr on each card still shows the real latest change. Germany is shown separately but is also part of the EU.

What the figures cover: electricity generated from solar, wind, hydropower, bioenergy and other renewables (such as geothermal and tidal). All adds these together. Heat and transport fuels are not included, and neither is nuclear power, which is low-carbon but not renewable. Share of its electricity is the source's share of all electricity that country generated in its latest official year.

The small line chart on each card shows that country's official annual generation from to , with the dot marking the latest year and the percentage giving the change over the whole period. Each chart is scaled to its own minimum and maximum (it does not start at zero), so compare the shape of the trend rather than the heights between countries. Tap Yearly data (or the chart) to see the values for each year.

Why it matters for the climate

Making electricity is the single biggest source of CO₂, mostly from burning coal and gas. Every megawatt-hour that comes from the sun, the wind or water instead of coal avoids roughly a tonne of CO₂ (about half that when it replaces gas).

Over their whole life, including building the panels, turbines and dams, wind, solar and hydro emit only a few percent of what coal does for the same electricity.

💡 If the Emissions Clock is the tap filling the bathtub, this clock shows one of the main ways we are turning the tap down: every unit of clean power is one that doesn't need to burn fuel.

Why the share matters as much as the total

Electricity demand keeps growing, so renewables only cut emissions when they grow faster than demand and push fossil fuels out of the mix. That's why each card shows the share of the country's electricity, not just the total.

Electricity is only part of the story

Electricity is about a fifth of the energy the world uses. Cars, heating, industry and planes still mostly burn fuel directly, which is why moving them onto clean electricity (electric cars, heat pumps) is the other half of the job.

Sources: Ember; Energy Institute; IPCC AR5 Annex III (life-cycle emissions per unit of electricity); IEA World Energy Outlook.
